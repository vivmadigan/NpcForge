**Merrin Voss, Innkeeper of The Brass Lantern**

Merrin Voss runs the Brass Lantern, a crowded, respectable inn wedged between the warehouse district and the city's noisier commercial streets. They are quick with a towel, quicker with a smile, and seem to know who arrived in town before the traveler has even finished taking off their coat.

Merrin is eager to seem useful, worldly, and connected. They nod along before anyone has quite finished speaking-sometimes so enthusiastically that they agree to things they plainly did not hear. A visitor with a crisp voice, fine coat, or merely enough confidence can make Merrin immediately attentive and accommodating.

At present, Merrin has an unpleasant obligation: they have been holding messages and small packages for a local fence, **Sella "Silk" Marrow**, in exchange for the fence ensuring the inn is left alone by certain rougher elements of the district. Merrin desperately wants out of the arrangement, but is afraid of what happens if they simply refuse.

They will not volunteer Sella's name. In fact, Merrin believes they are an excellent liar, despite becoming visibly strained whenever they attempt one: too many details, needless smiles, and a tendency to insist, "No, no, I've never heard of anyone like that," before the question is even fully asked.

If pressed for the fence's identity, Merrin initially stalls.

> "A fence? In my establishment? Certainly not. We serve travelers, merchants, artisans-honest people, mostly. Though, ah, some people do have. flexible definitions of honest."

A confident character can break through their resistance with little more than an authoritative assumption.

> "You're right. I should tell you. It's Sella Marrow-'Silk,' they call her. Keeps a back room above the Blue Eel chandlery, near the eastern docks. Don't tell her I sent you. Actually, do tell her. Tell her I was very helpful. But don't make it sound like I was frightened."

Merrin does not know how to reach the Blue Eel reliably and is quietly ashamed of this. They are terrible with directions, though they refuse to admit it. If asked how to get there, they give increasingly implausible instructions with great certainty.

> "Straight down Cinder Street, then take the second left after the statue. Or perhaps the statue is after the left. You'll know it when you see it-it's a very distinctive statue. Of. a man, I believe."
